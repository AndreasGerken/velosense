Map of surface roughness for bike rides

This map shows for every street in Berlin how rough it is on a bike, separately for each riding direction.

It works wherever rides are recorded with a phone on the bike: its accelerometer measures every bump, and GPS places it on the street. For now the rides come from the SimRa project, where cyclists record their rides with a phone app. It builds on OpenStreetMap to match the rides to the streets, and to carry what the rides show over to the surface and smoothness tags where there are not enough rides yet.

It is meant as an open building block: open code and open data, roughness per OpenStreetMap way and riding direction, on one scale for every street and city, for bike routers, cities and mappers to build on.

Green is smooth, red is rough, faint lines rest on few rides. Click or tap a street for its values. The views top left go from the raw rides to the values per street, the OSM tags and both merged.

A demo: all values are estimates and may contain errors or inaccuracies (wrongly matched rides, few rides, biases at low speed and on short segments). They are not a recommendation to change OSM tags. Feedback is welcome.

How it's calculated

Roughness

Every street is shown per riding direction (each on its own side). Roughness is "× as rough as good asphalt": ×1.5 shakes a bike 1.5 times as much as a street tagged surface=asphalt, smoothness=good at the same speed. A value ± x is its uncertainty (one standard error: the true value lies within it about 2 times in 3). Every view fades where its value is unsure: full up to ±15 %, faint at ±50 %.

Rides

What everything starts from: a sample of the SimRa rides (half at random, half picked so that quiet streets show too), each second's vertical shaking relative to the ride's own median (that takes out the phone, its mount and the bike). To keep the riders untraceable, the start and end of every ride and every other piece of it are left out.

Measured

From all SimRa rides. Each second of a ride gives the vertical shaking, corrected for speed and for the rider's phone and bike (the ride's median is taken out), averaged over 60 m of the ride: a short segment shows the street around it, not just a kerb at its end. The seconds are matched to the street network; the seconds of one ride on one street segment and direction form a pass. The estimate is the weighted mean of its passes.

Each ride is also compared with the other rides on its streets. A ride that reads rougher or smoother than the others all along is shifted back (its median can be off, e.g. on a ride over much cobblestone), and a ride whose values scatter more counts less.

The ± comes from how well the passes agree and how many there are, plus how much a street changes over time: even many older rides don't tell today's value closer than about ±11 %.

Data amount

The passes, each counted by its weight: age (a pass counts half at 3 years old and ×0.1 at 5), length (a 20 m pass counts 1, a longer one a little more), the matcher's certainty that the pass is on this street and not on a parallel one (0.5 for a cycle track exactly as close as the road) and how little the ride scatters. This is the information: 1 = one new, clearly matched 20 m pass of a typical ride.

OSM tags

What OpenStreetMap's surface and smoothness tags predict (surface:bicycle and smoothness:bicycle where a road has its own surface for bikes): the typical measured roughness of the streets with the same pair of tags (if at least 20 are well measured: information ≥ 3), else of all streets with the same surface. Without a surface tag nothing is predictable (grey). Its ± is how much these streets really differ, outliers included (read from the middle 80 % of them).

Combined

OSM and the measurement merged, each weighted by how sure it is (1 / uncertainty²). Without rides it is the OSM prediction, without an OSM prediction the measurement. Its ± follows from both: small where many rides passed, as large as the OSM one where none did, so a street known only from its tags shows faint.

Measured vs. OSM

Measured minus OSM, in units of their combined uncertainty (z). Beyond ±2 the street is clearly smoother (blue) or rougher (red) than its tags say.

Data

A demo: the values may contain errors and biases (wrongly matched rides, few rides, biases at low speed and on short segments). Please check them before you build on them.

  • roughness.csv.gz: every measured street segment and riding direction: its OSM way and nodes, tags, the measured, OSM and combined roughness with their ±, and its line (WKT, opens in QGIS).
  • tag_roughness.csv: per OSM surface and smoothness tag pair, the roughness the OSM view gives a street with it: a ready cost table for a bike router.
